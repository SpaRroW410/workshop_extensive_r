Region 1 · Which Test?

Matching the question and the data type to a test; reading t-test and chi-square output and p-values

Briefing

Read first: Test Selection Guide. The data types decide the test. A continuous outcome between two independent groups: two-sample t-test. Two categorical variables: chi-square test on their cross-table.

t.test(sbp ~ tobacco, data = ncd)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  sbp by tobacco
t = -3.8047, df = 333.3, p-value = 0.0001689
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group No and group Yes is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 -8.616917 -2.743400
sample estimates:
 mean in group No mean in group Yes 
         130.5810          136.2611 
chisq.test(table(ncd$residence, ncd$htn))

    Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction

data:  table(ncd$residence, ncd$htn)
X-squared = 2.2164e-30, df = 1, p-value = 1

Reading the output. The CI of a t-test is for the first group minus the second. A p-value is how surprising the data would be if there were no true difference: it is not the chance that the null hypothesis is true, and it says nothing about size or importance. A large p-value is not proof of no difference.

Level 1 · Pick the test

You want to compare the mean systolic BP of tobacco users and non-users. Which test is the usual starting point?

  • A chi-square test of SBP by tobacco use
  • A two-sample t-test comparing the two groups
  • A correlation between SBP and tobacco use
  • A paired t-test of each adult before and after

You want to know whether hypertension (yes/no) is more common in rural than in urban adults. Which test fits?

  • A chi-square test on the residence-by-htn table
  • A paired t-test of rural against urban adults
  • A two-sample t-test of hypertension by residence
  • A correlation of hypertension with residence codes

In a table of residence by hypertension, 122 of 350 rural adults are hypertensive. What percentage is that? (whole number)

Level 2 · Read the output

#>  Welch Two Sample t-test
#> data:  sbp by tobacco
#> t = -3.12, df = 412.6, p-value = 0.0019
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  -7.1 -1.6
#> sample estimates:
#>  mean in group No mean in group Yes
#>             127.2             131.6

Which reading is correct?

  • Non-users’ mean SBP is higher, as the t value is negative
  • The test shows that tobacco use raises SBP by 3.12 mmHg
  • There is a 0.19% chance that tobacco has no effect on SBP
  • Users’ mean SBP is higher; the CI for it excludes zero

A t-test comparing two groups gives p = 0.002. What does this mean?

  • There is a 0.2% probability that the two groups truly have equal means
  • A difference this large would be rare if there were no true difference
  • There is a 99.8% chance that the result will be repeated in a new sample
  • The difference between the group means is large and clinically important

A t-test of mean BMI between two villages gives p = 0.30. What is the best conclusion?

  • The two villages are shown to have exactly the same mean BMI
  • The test failed, so it should be repeated until p is below 0.05
  • Compatible with no difference, but not proof that there is none
  • There is a 30% chance that the two villages differ in mean BMI

Boss · Review the results

Boss: flag every flawed sentence in this results paragraph (cross-sectional survey, 600 adults).

  • Mean SBP was 131.6 mmHg in tobacco users and 127.2 mmHg in non-users (Welch t-test, p = 0.002).
  • Because p < 0.05, tobacco causes higher blood pressure.
  • Hypertension was more common in rural (35%) than urban (28%) adults (chi-square, p = 0.07), so residence makes no difference.
  • A t-test was used to compare the yes/no hypertension outcome between rural and urban adults.
  • Each result is reported with the test used, the group summaries and the exact p-value.
  • A p-value of 0.002 means there is a 0.2% chance that the result is due to chance.
