Placement check

10 short questions. Can you skip the basics? About 10 minutes, advisory only.

The game starts from the basics, so you never need this check. It is for people who already use R and want to skip ahead. It asks about the Region 1 skills (reading a dplyr pipeline, factors, plots, summary() tables, missing values). Results: Ready (skip to Region 3 · Regression), Nearly ready (brush up on a few library pages first) or Not yet (start at Region 1 · Basics). Not graded; nothing is locked.

ncd |> filter(age >= 50) |> summarise(n = n(), mean_sbp = mean(sbp))

What does this pipeline produce?

  • One row: the count of adults aged 50+ and their mean SBP
  • A new column called n added to each row of the data in ncd
  • One row per person, keeping only adults aged 50 or more in ncd
  • The mean SBP of everyone, with an extra column called n added
ncd |> group_by(sex) |> summarise(mean_sbp = mean(sbp, na.rm = TRUE))

How many rows does the result have if sex has two levels?

  • One: the overall mean SBP of all adults
  • 600: one value for each person
  • Two: the counts of women and men
  • Two: one mean SBP for each sex
table(ncd$sex)
#> female Female   Male 
#>     14    286    300

What is the problem, and what should you do before modelling?

  • Nothing: a model treats female and Female as the same level
  • One group is spelled two ways; recode it into two levels
  • The data has three sexes; add a third dummy variable
  • Males outnumber females, so the sample must be reweighted
#>             Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
#> (Intercept)  106.900      3.800   28.13   <2e-16
#> salt_g         3.400      0.420    8.10  3.1e-15

Which number is the estimated change in mean SBP per extra gram of salt?

  • 0.420, from the Std. Error column of the salt_g row
  • 8.10, from the t value column of the salt_g row
  • 106.900, from the Estimate column of the intercept
  • 3.400, from the Estimate column of the salt_g row
ggplot(ncd, aes(x = salt_g, y = sbp)) + geom_point() + geom_smooth(method = "lm")

What will you see?

  • A histogram of salt intake with the bars filled by SBP
  • A scatter of salt against SBP with a fitted line
  • A bar chart of the mean SBP in each salt-intake category
  • A box plot of SBP drawn separately for each person
mean(c(120, 130, NA))
#> [1] NA

Why is the result NA, and how would you get the mean of the two observed values?

  • The NA is dropped, so the output is simply wrong
  • R cannot add numbers stored in a vector; convert it to a list
  • The mean needs at least three non-missing values to work
  • A value is missing, so the mean is unknown; set na.rm = TRUE

In a model summary the salt row shows Pr(>|t|) = 3.1e-15. How should you read this p-value?

  • About 15%: not significant at the 5% level
  • About 3.1%: weak evidence against a zero slope
  • Not a probability; it is a count of 15 observations
  • Tiny: strong evidence against a zero slope
ncd |> mutate(htn_f = factor(htn, levels = 0:1, labels = c("No HTN", "HTN")))

What is the purpose of this step?

  • To make the model treat htn as a continuous measure
  • To label the 0/1 outcome so tables show readable names
  • To change the values of htn from 0 and 1 to 1 and 2 in the data
  • To remove the people with no hypertension from the data
