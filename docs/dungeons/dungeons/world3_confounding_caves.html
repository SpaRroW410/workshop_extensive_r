World 3 · Confounding Caves

Confounding, adjustment, significance vs importance, dummy variables, model building

Briefing

m_crude <- lm(sbp ~ salt_g, data = cc)
m_adj   <- lm(sbp ~ salt_g + age + bmi + sex + residence + education +
                tobacco + alcohol + phys_act, data = cc)
c(crude = coef(m_crude)[["salt_g"]], adjusted = coef(m_adj)[["salt_g"]])
   crude adjusted 
3.357640 1.799679

Confounding. A confounder is linked to the exposure and the outcome and is not on the path between them. Compare the crude and adjusted slope: a change of about 10% or more says the added variable matters. Adjusting removes only measured confounding.

Significance is not importance. A small p-value or a CI that excludes zero speaks to precision. Judge the size of the effect, in its units, against what is clinically meaningful. Do not adjust for mediators when you want the total effect.

Dummy variables. A factor with k levels becomes k − 1 dummies; each coefficient is a difference from the reference level, so always name the reference. To compare two non-reference levels, subtract their coefficients.

Building the model. Choose covariates from a causal diagram drawn before looking at results. Automatic stepwise selection is for exploration: its p-values and CIs are optimistic and the selected set is unstable.

Level 1 · Confounders and adjustment

Age is related to salt intake (older adults eat more salt) and to SBP, and age does not lie on the path from salt to SBP. In the salt → SBP analysis, age is a…

  • precision variable that changes nothing
  • confounder that should be adjusted for
  • collider that should be conditioned on
  • mediator that should be left out of the model

The crude salt slope is 3.0 mmHg per g/day. After adjusting for age and BMI it is 1.8. What percentage of the crude slope was due to confounding? (whole number)

In a registry of 50,000 adults, each extra gram of salt goes with 0.3 mmHg higher SBP (95% CI 0.2–0.4, p < 0.001). The best reading is that…

  • salt is clearly irrelevant because the slope is below 1
  • the CI excludes zero, so the effect is clinically important
  • the association is precise, but 0.3 mmHg is clinically tiny
  • the effect is large because the p-value is very small

Level 2 · Mediators and dummy variables

Suppose salt causes fluid retention, which in turn raises SBP. A student adjusts for fluid retention when estimating the total effect of salt. What is the likely result?

  • The salt slope is unbiased because more adjustment is better
  • The salt slope is unchanged because mediators rarely matter
  • The salt slope grows because the model is more precise
  • The salt slope shrinks because part of its effect is blocked

BMI category is coded with ‘Normal’ as reference. Overweight has β = 3.2 and Obese has β = 7.0 mmHg. How many mmHg higher is mean SBP in Obese than in Overweight, other variables fixed? (1 decimal)

Education has five levels: none, primary, secondary, graduate and postgraduate. How many dummy variables enter the model? (whole number)

Level 3 · Building a model

A resident runs automatic stepwise selection (AIC) on 25 candidate variables and reports the final model’s p-values as confirmatory. What is the main problem?

  • Stepwise selection cannot be run on a continuous outcome
  • The AIC value is valid when fewer than ten variables are tested
  • The p-values and CIs are too optimistic and the set is unstable
  • Automatic selection always keeps every confounder in the model

Put a defensible, theory-driven way of choosing covariates in order, from first to last:

  1. Fix the model in the analysis plan before looking at results
  2. Fit crude and adjusted models and compare the exposure slope
  3. List the confounders; leave out mediators and colliders
  4. Draw the causal diagram from subject knowledge
  5. State the exposure, the outcome and the study question
  6. Report the assumed diagram and a sensitivity analysis

Boss · Rebuild a confounded analysis

Boss: flag every flawed sentence in this write-up (cross-sectional survey, n = 580; crude salt slope 3.0, adjusted 1.8 mmHg per g/day).

  • Salt intake was associated with higher SBP: 1.8 mmHg per g/day (95% CI 1.2–2.4) after adjusting for age, sex, BMI and residence.
  • Because the slope fell from 3.0 to 1.8, age and BMI were removed from the analysis as non-significant.
  • The adjusted analysis proves that cutting salt will lower SBP by 1.8 mmHg per gram.
  • Residence was entered as one dummy variable, with Urban as the stated reference.
  • Covariates were chosen from a diagram drawn before analysis, not from p-values.
  • Since the model adjusts for confounders, no confounding can remain.
