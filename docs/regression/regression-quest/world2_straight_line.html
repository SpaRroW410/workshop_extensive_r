World 2 · The Straight Line

Slope in units, prediction, CI vs PI, residuals, R², centering

Briefing

m1 <- lm(sbp ~ salt_g, data = ncd)
round(coef(m1), 2)
(Intercept)      salt_g 
     107.42        3.39

Slope in units. The slope is the difference in mean SBP (mmHg) per extra gram of daily salt, across people. The intercept is the mean at zero, often outside the data.

new_salt <- tibble(salt_g = c(5, 8, 12))
cbind(salt_g = new_salt$salt_g,
      fit = round(predict(m1, new_salt), 1),
      CI = apply(round(predict(m1, new_salt, interval = "confidence")[, 2:3], 0), 1, paste, collapse = "–"),
      PI = apply(round(predict(m1, new_salt, interval = "prediction")[, 2:3], 0), 1, paste, collapse = "–"))
  salt_g fit     CI        PI       
1 "5"    "124.4" "123–126" "95–154" 
2 "8"    "134.5" "133–136" "105–164"
3 "12"   "148.1" "145–151" "118–178"

CI vs PI. The confidence interval is about the mean of a group; the prediction interval is about one new person and includes the residual scatter, so it is far wider.

r2(m1)
# R2 for Linear Regression
       R2: 0.202
  adj. R2: 0.200

R² vs adjusted R². R² never falls when variables are added; adjusted R² charges a penalty per parameter. Centring (for example age at 50) makes the intercept the expected outcome at a meaningful value without changing the slope.

Level 1 · Slope, prediction, residuals

A model gives sbp = 107.4 + 3.4 × salt_g. What does the 3.4 mean?

  • Daily salt accounts for 3.4 mmHg of each person’s own SBP
  • Mean SBP is 3.4 mmHg higher per extra gram of daily salt
  • Each extra gram of daily salt raises SBP by 3.4 percent
  • Each person’s SBP rises 3.4 mmHg whenever their daily salt rises 1 g

Using sbp = 107.4 + 3.4 × salt_g, what mean SBP (mmHg, nearest whole number) is predicted at 10 g/day?

A man eating 10 g/day has SBP 150 mmHg while the line predicts 141.4. What is his residual (observed − fitted)?

Level 2 · Intervals and R²

At 8 g/day the 95% CI for mean SBP is 133–136 but the 95% prediction interval is 105–164. Why is the second so much wider?

  • Prediction intervals are built from the sample range directly
  • It adds person-to-person scatter to the uncertainty of the line
  • Prediction intervals are computed at a higher confidence level than 95%
  • The line is less precisely estimated at 8 g than at 10 g
r2(lm(sbp ~ salt_g))           # R2: 0.202   adj. R2: 0.200
r2(lm(sbp ~ salt_g + z1:z20))  # R2: 0.227   adj. R2: 0.198

z1:z20 are twenty random-noise columns. What should you conclude?

  • Adjusted R² must exceed R², so one of the lines is wrong
  • An R² near 0.2 means the salt model is of no use
  • The twenty extra variables clearly helped because R² rose by 0.025
  • The noise columns did not help: adjusted R² did not improve

Level 3 · Centring

Age spans 30–69 years and the intercept of sbp ~ age is 96 mmHg, which describes nobody. What makes it interpretable?

  • Standardise SBP so that its mean becomes zero
  • Centre age at 50 so the intercept is expected SBP at 50
  • Delete everyone younger than 30 from the dataset
  • Multiply age by 100 so that the slope looks much bigger

Put the steps for a centred model in order, from first to last:

  1. Choose a meaningful reference value such as age 50
  2. Read the intercept as the mean outcome at the reference value
  3. Refit the model using the centred variable
  4. Create the centred variable by subtracting that value
  5. Confirm that the slope did not change

Boss · Interpretation inspector

Boss: flag every flawed sentence about sbp = 107 + 3.4 × salt_g (R² = 0.20, n = 582, cross-sectional survey).

  • Each extra gram of daily salt goes with 3.4 mmHg higher mean SBP.
  • The model accounts for about 20% of the variability in SBP.
  • An adult eating 0 g of salt would have an SBP of exactly 107 mmHg.
  • Adding 5 g of salt to one patient’s diet will raise that patient’s SBP by 17 mmHg.
  • The 95% prediction interval at 8 g/day is narrower than the 95% CI for the mean.
  • Because R² is only 0.20, the salt association is clinically unimportant.
